# Retro Game Sourcing — NZ Import Playbook

**Compiled:** 16 July 2026
**Refresh cadence:** quarterly (shops churn; levies and fees change)
**Priorities (ranked):** 1. Authenticity · 2. Landed cost · 3. Catalogue breadth · 4. Speed

---

## 0. Read this first — the three rules that matter more than the shop list

Which shop you buy from matters less than **how the parcel crosses the border**. Three
mechanics dominate landed cost, and two of them are counter-intuitive.

### Rule 1 — Small shops are legitimately GST-free. Big ones aren't.

Since Dec 2019, overseas sellers must register for and charge 15% NZ GST **only if** their
sales to NZ exceed **NZ$60,000/year**. Below $1,000 consignment value, Customs does not
collect GST at the border either.

So the chain is:

| Seller | NZ sales > $60k? | GST outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Amazon, eBay, AliExpress | Yes | 15% charged at checkout |
| Small US/UK specialist retro shop | Almost certainly no | **No GST at all** |

This **inverts** the usual assumption that the big retailer is cheaper. A niche retro shop
in Pennsylvania is very unlikely to clear NZ$60k/yr of NZ-specific sales, which means a
legal 15% saving versus the same cart bought via eBay. This is not a loophole — it's the
design of the LVIG regime.

### Rule 2 — Never let a consignment cross NZ$1,000.

Above NZ$1,000 (customs value, **excluding** shipping) you trigger all of:

- 15% GST on (goods + freight + insurance + duty)
- Customs duty (often 0% under FTAs, but assessed)
- **High-value goods levy: ~NZ$51.81** (air) — new from 1 April 2026
- Standard Import Declaration processing

The jump from $999 to $1,001 is roughly a 20% cost cliff. Split orders. But note the
**aggregation trap**: consignments from the *same supplier* arriving on the *same day*
are combined and counted as one. Stagger them.

### Rule 3 — The NZ$400 YouShop dead zone.

NZ Post's You Shop charges **NZ$55 incl. GST per parcel** for tariff classification on any
parcel valued **NZ$400–$1,000**. Combined with Rule 2, this leaves a clean operating band:

> **Target parcel value: under NZ$400. Consolidate up to that, then ship.**

Below $400: no classification fee, no GST, no duty. Above $400: $55. Above $1,000: the cliff.

### Also new (April 2026)

A **Low-Value Goods levy** of **NZ$2.21 + GST per consignment** (air; $2.09 sea) now applies
to *every* import ≤NZ$1,000. Small, but it mildly penalises a many-tiny-parcels strategy —
which pushes you further toward "consolidate to just under $400."

### The consolidation tension

Freight forwarding saves a lot on shipping — but consolidation pushes parcel value up, toward
the $400 fee and the $1,000 cliff. **These pull in opposite directions.** The $400 band is
where they balance.

## 2. Tier B — Australia ⭐ **This is the strongest tier for you**

**Why AU wins on your ranking:** it's **PAL — same region as NZ**. Carts work on stock NZ
hardware with no mod, no converter, no 50/60Hz argument. Fully Retro states outright that
their games are compatible with Australian systems, which means NZ systems too. Add short
freight, and small AU sellers almost certainly sit under the NZ$60k GST threshold (Rule 1).

That combination — authenticity + no region risk + no GST + cheap freight — hits priorities
1 and 2 simultaneously. **Australia should be your default, not the US.**

### The Fully Retro vs Retro Sales tell

Retro Sales hosts a page titled **"Buy Retro Games Australia — Without the Fully Retro Prices."**
Competitors don't build landing pages targeting a rival's pricing unless that rival is
reliably more expensive. That's the same premium-vs-value split as DKOldies vs Lukie in the
US — except here it's stated out loud.

**Practical read:** price-check Retro Sales first, use Fully Retro's free NZ shipping as the
tiebreaker on small orders (free freight can erase a modest price gap on a single cart).

**Why Japan matters for your #1 priority:** reproduction carts overwhelmingly target the
*Western* collector market — that's where the money and the naïve buyers are. Japanese-region
Famicom/Super Famicom/Mega Drive carts circulating in the Japanese domestic second-hand
economy have a structurally lower repro rate. Buying JP-region from JP marketplaces is one
of the stronger authenticity plays available — **if** you can play the carts (§6) and **read
them (§4a)**.

### 4a. ⚠️ Can you play Japanese carts in English? Mostly **no**.

This is the constraint that decides whether Japan is worth the plumbing at all.

Retro cartridges are **not** like modern games. There's no multi-language build with a
language-select screen — a Super Famicom cart is a physically *different ROM* from the SNES
cart, compiled for one market, in one language. Whatever's burned into the mask ROM is what
you get, forever.

So it splits hard by genre:

| Verdict | Genres | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ **Buy freely** | Shmups/STG, fighting, racing, sports, puzzle | Near text-free. Menus in Japanese are a 5-minute learning curve, not a barrier. |
| ⚠️ **Usually fine** | Platformers, action | Minimal text — Super Mario World, F-Zero and similar are effectively language-independent. |
| ❌ **Don't** | **RPGs**, visual novels, strategy, adventure | Wall-to-wall Japanese text. JP-region *Final Fantasy VI* is unreadable without Japanese. |

**The cruel irony:** the Japanese library's crown jewels — the Super Famicom RPG catalogue —
are exactly the games you can't read. The genre that most justifies importing is the genre
most locked behind the language.

**The genuine exception — Sega Mega Drive.** Sega developed much of its library with Western
release in mind, so a lot of JP-region Mega Drive games display **English text anyway**
(Sonic, Streets of Rage and similar). Mega Drive is comfortably the most English-friendly
Japanese platform. Treat this as title-by-title, not a blanket rule — verify per game before
buying.

**Strategy that falls out of this:**

- Wantlist is **shmups / fighters** → Japan is your best authenticity-per-dollar source. Go hard.
- Wantlist is **Mega Drive** → Japan is viable across most genres. Check per title.
- Wantlist is **RPGs** → Japan is off the table. Fall back to Tier B (AU/PAL). The plumbing
  in §5 isn't worth building.

## 5. The plumbing — proxies and forwarders

### Japan proxies

The fee structure is the durable fact; the exact yen figures are **not**. The comparison
sites are affiliate-driven and actively contradict each other (I saw ZenMarket quoted at
both ¥300 and ¥500/item, and Neokyo at ¥250, ¥300, *and* ¥350, all within 2026 sources).
**Confirm live rates before any large order.**

| Service | Model | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **ZenMarket** | Flat ~¥300/item, free consolidation, 45–60d storage | Multi-item hauls; anything mid-to-high value. Flat fee wins as price rises. |
| **Buyee** | ~5–6% + flat | Official Mercari/Yahoo/Rakuma partnerships. Best auto-bidding for Yahoo Auctions. Beginner-friendly. Percentage overtakes ZenMarket's flat fee somewhere around ¥5,000–6,000/item. |
| **Neokyo** | Flat (~¥250–350, disputed) | EU-optimised rates — **less relevant for NZ**. Manual auction bidding = slow on competitive lots. |
| **FROM JAPAN** | Flat ~¥250 + 5% consolidation | Amazon JP; mid-high value single items. |
| **Sendico** | — | 90-day storage — longest, good for slow-building hauls. |

**Practical split:** many buyers run both — Buyee for Yahoo Auctions (sniping), ZenMarket
for everything else (flat fee + free consolidation).

### US/UK freight forwarders

| Service | Notes |
|---|---|
| **NZ Post You Shop** | US **and** UK addresses. NZ-native, integrated declaration flow. **Watch the $55 tariff classification fee on $400–1,000 parcels** (§Rule 3). Also aggregates multiple <$400 parcels sent simultaneously to the same address — deliberate splitting can backfire. |
| **Stackry** | US. Warehouse in a **tax-free state** → saves ~7.5% US sales tax on top of everything else. Consolidation, de-boxing to cut volumetric weight, 45 days free storage. Claims up to 80% freight saving. **Strong candidate — the sales-tax saving stacks with the GST saving from Rule 1.** |
| **MyUS / Shipito / Planet Express** | US. Unverified this pass. |
| **Forward2Me** | UK. Unverified this pass. Relevant for PAL-region sourcing (§6). |

**Volumetric weight matters:** You Shop uses `L×W×H / 5000`. Boxed retro games are mostly
air. De-boxing services (Stackry) directly attack this — but destroy collector value on
CIB items. Loose carts: de-box freely. CIB: never.

## 6. ⚠️ The unresolved question: region

**NZ is PAL.** This runs straight through every decision above and I've assumed nothing.

- **US shops (Tier C)** sell **NTSC-U** carts. These need an NTSC console, a region mod, or
  a converter. On a PAL SNES, NTSC carts need the tab mod *and* still hit 50/60Hz issues.
- **Japan (§4)** is **NTSC-J**. Super Famicom carts physically fit a SNES after tab removal,
  but region lockout and timing still apply.
- **AU/UK (Tier B, Forward2Me)** are **PAL** — plug-and-play for a stock NZ console. But PAL
  releases are often 50Hz, letterboxed, and slower than NTSC.

**This changes the ranking of the entire list.** If you're running stock PAL hardware, Tier B
and UK sources jump above the US shops. If you're running modded/region-free hardware or
CRT-with-multi-standard, the US and Japan open up and Japan becomes the authenticity winner.

If you're chasing *authenticity* as defined by "the cart is a real factory cart" — region is
irrelevant. If you mean "authentic to how I played it as a kid in NZ" — that's PAL, and it's
a much narrower and often more expensive market.

**I need your answer on this before the list can be properly ranked.**
